Separating Graph and Policy Effects in Adaptive
Mathematics
Sequencing: A Simulation Study
Phạm Triều Dương, Hồ Nguyễn Minh Sang, and Lê Văn Tuấn
Faculty of Software Engineering
University of Information Technology, VNU-HCM
Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam
{23520355, 23521338}@student.uit.edu.vn, tuanlv@uit.edu.vn
Abstract—Prerequisite-aware adaptive systems can restrict
learners to ready concepts yet still generate fragmented paths
because graph feasibility does not determine which ready concept
is selected. This paper contributes a controlled decomposition of
these two mechanisms in Grade 10 mathematics: prerequisite-
graph structure and selection policy. We implement an inter-
pretable sequencer that combines a directed concept graph,
concept-level Elo estimates, closest-difficulty item selection, and
chapter continuity. We first compare a fixed linear baseline, a
25-edge intra-chapter graph, and a 31-edge advanced graph under
the same continuity-aware controller, using 50 seeds for each of
three synthetic learner profiles. We then hold the advanced graph
fixed and ablate three selection policies. For the uniformly strong
and function-weak profiles, both graph conditions reduce mean
chapter switches from 11 to 2 and re-entry from 9 to 0; reductions
are smaller for the uniformly weak profile. More importantly,
continuity yields zero re-entry in every tested run, whereas the
two non-continuity policies average 16.70–19.36 re-entries across
profiles. The advanced graph shows no consistent advantage over
the standard graph, so its added relations cannot be credited with
an independent gain. The result shows why prerequisite feasibility
and path continuity should be designed and evaluated separately.
These findings characterize simulated mechanism behavior, not
classroom effectiveness, and require validation with real learners.
Index Terms—adaptive learning, concept graph, educational
technology, Elo rating, learning-path sequencing
I. INTRODUCTION
Mathematics is cumulative: a missing prerequisite can
hinder a topic in another chapter. In Grade 10, set operations,
quadratic inequalities, and quadratic functions form connected
dependencies [1]. Yet systems that personalize pace or difficulty
may retain a common content order, causing repeated chapter
changes or premature exposure. Adaptive systems instead use
learner state and domain structure to select material at each
interaction [2], [3].
A prerequisite graph determines which concepts are feasible,
but the selection policy determines which feasible concept
appears next. We study an interpretable sequencer in which a
directed graph constrains feasibility, concept-level Elo estimates
mastery, and a continuity-aware controller prefers the current
chapter. Elo supports inexpensive online updates [4], while con-
cept graphs represent prerequisites explicitly [5]. The method is
transparent sequencing rather than a learned prediction model.
We ask three research questions.RQ1:Under one shared
continuity-aware controller, how do a fixed linear sequence
(B0), a standard intra-chapter graph (B1), and an advanced
graph (GA) differ in switching, re-entry, and mastery?RQ2:
Does GA provide a consistent advantage over B1 in the
current concept set?RQ3:With GA held fixed, how does
chapter-continuity selection affect re-entry relative to plain
lowest-Elo and cross-chapter-unlock policies? This two-stage
design compares structural conditions and then isolates policy
behavior; all outcomes are simulation evidence rather than
classroom effectiveness.
The paper makes three contributions:
• a controlled evaluation design that separates prerequisite-
graph structure from the policy that selects among feasible
concepts;
• reproducible evidence across three synthetic profiles and
50 seeds, including the negative result that GA does not
consistently improve on B1 and an ablation attributing zero
re-entry to chapter continuity; and
• an interpretable sequencing artifact over 28 Grade 10
mathematics concepts that combines prerequisite readiness,
concept-level Elo updates, closest-difficulty item selection,
and chapter continuity.
The remainder reviews related work, defines the method, reports
results, and discusses limitations.
II. RELATEDWORK
A. Adaptive Sequencing and Learner Modeling
Adaptive systems combine domain, learner, and policy
models. Adaptive hypermedia pioneered user-model-based
navigation [2], [6], and personalized path guidance jointly
considered difficulty and concept continuity [7], while tutoring
systems demonstrated structured practice and feedback [3].
Learner-modeling reviews distinguish knowledge representation
from the policy acting on it [8]. Sequencing research studies
the policy directly [9], [10]; decision-theoretic work further
relates teaching actions to the learner model, domain, and

objective [11]. Thus, a valid state and graph do not prevent
alternating among feasible chapters.
Knowledge tracing estimates evolving mastery from inter-
actions [12]; deep knowledge tracing learns flexible temporal
representations from student data [13], while factorization
approaches support sparse data and side information [14].
Broader comparisons also cover Bayesian and logistic learner
models [15]. Elo provides a lightweight online estimate on a
shared learner–item scale [4]. Here, Elo is only a concept-level
state estimator: difficulties stay fixed and no model is trained
from the simulations. We evaluate sequencing behavior, not
predictive superiority.
B. Concept and Knowledge Graphs
A prerequisite graph uses directed edges to restrict selection
until parent concepts are mastered while permitting multiple
valid routes. Knowledge-space theory related learner states to
graph-like structures [16]; later work learned concept graphs
and prerequisite relations from educational data [5], [17], [18].
Knowledge-graph systems also organize adaptive resources and
paths [19]. These works motivate structured domains, but a
controller must still choose among ready concepts.
C. Research Gap
Prior work establishes state estimation, prerequisites, and
policy optimization, but treating them as one intervention
obscures whether path behavior comes from the feasible set
or the controller’s choice. We reportchapter switchesand the
stricterchapter re-entryin two stages: a structural comparison
under one controller, then a policy ablation on one graph.
The contribution is not a new graph-learning, tracing, or
optimization algorithm, but a reproducible mechanism analysis
that attributes continuity to the responsible component and
retains the negative result when the advanced graph does not
outperform the standard one. A mathematics teacher reviewed
the domain artifacts.
III. PROPOSEDMETHOD
A. System Overview
The adaptive engine maintains an Elo estimate θv,t for every
concept v at interaction t. A directed graph restricts selection to
concepts whose prerequisites are mastered. The controller then
favors a ready concept in the learner’s current chapter, selects
an item of nearby difficulty, observes a binary response, and
updates the corresponding Elo estimate. Fig. 1 summarizes this
closed loop. The components are deliberately interpretable: the
graph determines feasibility, the policy determines navigation,
and Elo updates only the estimated learner state.

IV. EXPERIMENTALEVALUATION
A. Simulation Setup
The evaluation separates graph structure from selection
policy. All conditions use the same 84-item bank, concept-level
Elo update, fixed item difficulties, mastery threshold τ= 1250 ,
dynamic K, and 500-interaction budget. Only the graph or the
stated selection policy changes within a comparison.
We use uniformly strong (US), uniformly weak (UW),
and function-weak (FW) synthetic profiles. US samples
concept ability from N(1400,50 2); UW uses N(1050,50 2).
FW uses N(1000,50 2) for quadratic-function concepts and
N(1250,50 2) elsewhere. The engine’s observable Elo estimates
are distinct from these latent values: estimates start at 1200,
except that FW starts at 1050 in the quadratic-functions unit.
A response is sampled from the logistic probability in (5)
using latent ability, and the latent value increases by 12 after
each item with feedback, up to 1500. This learning gain is a
simulation assumption, not an empirical estimate.
The structural comparison contains three models. B0 follows
a fixed lexicographic topological order. B1 uses the 25-
edge intra-chapter graph, and GA uses the 31-edge advanced
graph. The graph models share the continuity-aware lowest-
Elo controller. We run 50 seeds for every persona–model pair,
producing 450 runs. Achapter switchis any change from the

B. Graph-Structure Comparison
Table I reports aggregate outcomes. Under the continuity-
aware controller, B1 and GA reduce mean switches from 11
to 2 and mean re-entry from 9 to 0 for US and FW. For UW,
switches fall from 2.940 to 1 and re-entry from 0.940 to 0.
UW often reaches the 500-interaction cap: its mastery rates
therefore describe progress within a fixed budget, not eventual
mastery.
Fig. 2 shows that most groups are deterministic with respect
to the switch count; only UW under B0 varies across seeds.
The main finding is therefore about the combined graph-and-
controller conditions relative to the fixed sequence. GA and B1
are almost identical on switching and mastery. Because GA
also adds three intra-chapter relations alongside three cross-
chapter relations, these data neither isolate nor demonstrate an
independent advantage from cross-chapter edges.
C. Selection-Strategy Ablation
To identify the source of the re-entry reduction, we hold GA
fixed and compare continuity-aware lowest-Elo, plain lowest-
Elo, and a cross-chapter-unlock strategy. Fig. 3 reports the
result. Continuity produces zero re-entry in every tested run
for all three personas. Plain lowest-Elo averages 16.70, 17.22,
and 18.36 re-entries for US, UW, and FW, respectively; cross-
chapter unlock averages 16.86, 17.26, and 19.36.

D. Threats to Validity
A mathematics teacher reviewed the domain artifacts. Gaus-
sian personas compress learner diversity into three profiles,
item responses follow the same logistic family used by the
estimator, and the latent ability gain of 12 is assumed. The
finite question bank omits richer item characteristics, and the
simulator does not model forgetting, motivation, disengagement,
or measurement noise beyond response sampling. GA differs
from B1 by three intra-chapter and three cross-chapter relations,
preventing a cross-edge-only causal claim. Finally, repeated
seeds quantify stochastic stability within this simulator but
do not represent a student population. The evaluation tests
navigation mechanisms, not educational effectiveness.
V. DISCUSSION ANDCONCLUSION
A. Interpretation and Limitations
The results answer the three research questions with de-
liberately limited claims. For RQ1, prerequisite-constrained
sequencing with chapter continuity substantially reduces chap-
ter switching and eliminates re-entry in the tested profiles
relative to the fixed baseline. Mastery remains comparable
for US and FW. The UW outcomes are conditional on the
500-interaction budget and do not establish eventual mastery.
For RQ2, the advanced graph does not consistently improve
on the standard intra-chapter graph. Their navigation measures
are identical, and their mastery rates are similar across the
simulated profiles. Moreover, GA adds three intra-chapter
relations together with three cross-chapter relations, so the
experiment cannot isolate the effect of cross-chapter edges.
The negative result is useful: adding structural detail is not
automatically beneficial.
For RQ3, holding GA fixed reveals that chapter continuity,
rather than the graph alone, causes the zero-re-entry behavior.
Plain lowest-Elo and cross-chapter-unlock selection average
between 16.70 and 19.36 re-entries, whereas the continuity-
aware controller produces none. This distinction is central
to interpreting graph-based adaptive systems: prerequisites
determine which concepts are feasible, while the controller
determines the experienced path.
These findings concern mechanism behavior in simulation,
not educational effectiveness. One teacher reviewed the domain
artifacts; broader review remains desirable. The learner profiles
cover only three synthetic patterns, and the response and
learning processes are simplified. Future work should broaden
this review, add forgetting and backward remediation, test cross-

B. Conclusion
This paper presented an interpretable adaptive mathematics
sequencer that combines a prerequisite graph, concept-level Elo
estimates, and a continuity-aware selection policy. Across 450
simulated runs, the combined graph-and-controller conditions
reduced fragmented navigation without a consistent advantage
for the advanced graph over the standard graph. The ablation
further showed that chapter continuity—not cross-chapter graph
edges—eliminated re-entry in the tested setting. The study
therefore provides a reproducible modeling result and a cautious
basis for broader educator review and learner studies.
